## Grant application can take over your life

Grant writing takes a lot of time and energy. It can consume your life. Even when you are taking a much-needed break, you might feel, "You should be writing".

I have forgotten about this feeling for a while. When I was doing a PhD, I felt this a lot. My challenge was to manage that feeling—that I had to convince myself that taking breaks from writing is ok. Although I've never found a way to successfully eliminate this feeling, I have learned to accept that it's ok not to work all the time.

But with a set deadline, it's hard not to feel that way.

In the past weeks, I found my life consumed by the grant application. I wanted to write about my learning.

## InfraCoalition View

The grant proposal is called [InfraCoalition](https://www.researchequals.com/modules/ybww-tn1n). The proposal is for the [Open Science Netherlands Infrastructure Call](https://www.openscience.nl/en/calls/open-science-infrastructure). In the proposal, we the applicants want to transform an existing publishing platform ([ResearchEquals](https://www.researchequals.com/)) to an open infrastructure that does not depend on Big Tech, bringing power back to the research communities.

The current research infrastructures are mostly centralized. The business models of [Pure](https://www.elsevier.com/products/pure), [Scopus](https://www.elsevier.com/products/scopus), and most publishers focus on bringing researchers to one place to their platform. Scientific power is being centralized. Worse, they will track your behavior on the platform and sell them for extra profit.

The centralization is problematic since it does not reflect the decentralized nature of academia. The academia is inherently decentralized and federated. Universities are independent. But, they collectively advance the human knowledge together.

It's time for the research communities to reclaim autonomy in the research infrastructure. I believe InfraCoalition can help with that.

## Writing Process and Learning

The writing process of the grant is always hard, and InfraCoalition is not an exception.

We started with a great [pre-proposal](https://www.researchequals.com/modules/ybww-tn1n) spearheaded by [Chris Hartgerink](https://www.chjh.nl/). The proposal was received well, reviewers gave positive advice to submit a full proposal.

In the main proposal writing phase, I took the main driver's seat. It was my first time to coordinate a coalition of a six.

Chris helped me a lot on this. Below were some points of learning for me.

### 1. Trust that everyone is there to help you

In the first meeting, I was nervous whether I would do good for bringing people together. I was too focused on having a perfect meeting acing it as a chair. But the reality is that everyone is there to help you during the meeting. Everyone wants a successful meeting. The chair's responsibility is to create clear moments for members to contribute.

For me, the challenge is to state my opinion clearly without hesitation. Coming from a Japanese culture, I find it difficult to state my opinion directly. For example, when we make a certain decision, I tend to introduce the topic neutrally without stating my opinion and hear from the room.

I found that this approach adds unnecessary confusion in the process.

Being the first one to express an opinion in a group is scary. The chair can take the bullet to lift the task from others.

### 2: Ask timeline for every request for action

When requesting information or action (for example, to review something), it's better to put a date first. It's not a hard deadline, but it's an ask so that all us can plan.

### 3: Focus on co-creation

Working on a material together is a challenge. You may create something. And someone else will completely re-write the thing. Perhaps a common experience for a PhD candidate where their supervisor re-writes paragraphs in the publication.

I think this is ok. The important thing is that we should separate the text and the idea in it. The text may be deleted or altered. But your idea that you had influenced the second writer to come up with the paragraphs.

### 4: Call for a vote when things are not clear

In a group meeting, it's often unclear whether everyone agrees on certain topics or not. The meeting chair can ask the room, "Does everyone agree with this?". Some may react nodding, and others may say something, but it's hard if we have a unanimous decision. In cases like this, the chair can actively call for a vote by raising hands. For example, saying "I want to take a vote to check our stance on this as a group".

### 5: Making sure that everyone's voices are included

Sometimes, some members of the group speak more than others. The chair's responsibility is not to quiet them, but to make sure that everyone expressed their opinion. The chair can say something like, "I want to make sure that everyone had a chance to express their opinion. Is anyone who hasn't spoken up wants to contribute?"

## tl;dr

I joined the [Open Science Retreat](https://open.science-retreat.org/) 2025 in Beatenberg, Switzerland. I came there with a little wish to create a pub quiz with an Open Science theme. Little did I know the great adventure that I undertake with my teammates.

## Proposing creating a pub quiz

Open Science Retreat is an unconference without a set agenda. Participants propose topics on the spot. I suggested creating a pub quiz about Open Science, as a fun way to learn about the topics. The world needs more knowledge about Open Science and fun, after all. Below is the slide that I created to pitch my idea.

## From a pub quiz to a "Family Feud" game

[Melanie Imming](https://www.immingimpact.eu/), [Mariana Menegon](https://orcid.org/0009-0000-4655-5528), and later [Jazelle Carillo](https://jazellemaira.design/), joined the group with me. And I was not prepared for the good ride. Melanie pointed out that a pub quiz question always has a correct answer. But the world of Open Science is full of "it depends". For example, when asked about the most important value in Open Science, some may answer reproducibility, and others may say knowledge equity ([Fecher & Friesike, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-00026-8_2)). Both of these answers are correct as being voices in the community. We wanted a game that can celebrate different perspective and facilitate conversations about Open Science.

We found the format of [Family Feud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_Feud) suitable. In Family Feud, the organizer does a survey to a set of questions to the audience (for example, "Name a house that you never want to be in"). Then, the contestants' task is to guess the popular answers (for example, "Haunted House"). We thought we can use this format to highlight different voices of the Open Science community.

## Creating Survey Says: Open Science!

We decided to play the game on the last night of the retreat with the retreat participants. So, we only had about 2.5 days to prepare. We named the game, "Survey Says: Open Science!" and started preparing.

### Creating Survey Questions

The first task was to create survey questions. We came up with the following questions:

1. Open Science is driven by \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

1. In order to make Science more open, we need to reward \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

1. The most hurtful consequence of Gold Open Access is \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

1. I like my community to be \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

1. \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ is the most important value for Open Science

1. Please talk \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ to me!

1. University rankings are good for \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

1. Open Science, just Science \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

1. Citizens play the role of \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ in Science

Melanie created a Typeform survey, and we asked people to answer it:

[![Melanie's toot asking people to answer the survey](melanie-toot.jpeg)](https://akademienl.social/@meliimming/114337114172005307)

In the short time, we received 77 responses. Thank you to those who responded to the survey!

### Grouping and Coding Survey Answers

We could not use the survey responses as-is because people can write in answers in slightly different ways. For example, for the question, "In order to make science more open, we need to reward \_\_(blank)\_\_", people answered "data sharing" and "code sharing". They are both about Open Science practices, so we grouped them into a bigger category, "OS practices (data / code sharing, preregistration)".

The challenge was to determine how to group answers together. We wanted to avoid having too many groups, but also making answers too generic. There was no one way to do it. And I believe if others try to code the same data, there may be other ways to group. That's an opportunity to enjoy the game with the same survey responses but different groupings.

### Designing for the Day - Bidding System

Usually, Family Feud is a game for two groups of contestants (two families). But, we at the Survey Says team wanted anyone in the retreat attendee participate in the game. To achieve this goal, we set up a bidding system. Attendees first formed groups. Then, all groups received virtual money ("question professing fee"). For each round, the groups placed a bid, so that the group with the highest bid can answer the question. If they answer wrong 3 times, it's a strike, and the team with the next highest bid got to answer the question.
